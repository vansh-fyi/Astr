The graph on the atmospherics sheet covers one night, from sunset to the next sunrise, with five time labels across the bottom.

**The moon curve** is the moon's altitude multiplied by its illuminated fraction when it is above the horizon, sampled every 15 minutes. Its unit is degrees times a fraction, which has no physical meaning beyond "higher and fuller is worse". Because it is plotted against 90, a full moon at the zenith reaches the top and a half moon at 60° reaches a third of the height.

</DocSection>

<DocSection id="prime-view" title="Prime view">

Prime view is the best stretch of the night for observing. For each hourly sample in the night:

$$ \text{score} = 0.7\,\frac{c}{100} + 0.3\,\frac{i\,a}{90} $$

where $c$ is the cloud cover in percent, $i$ the moon's illuminated fraction and $a$ its altitude in degrees when above the horizon (otherwise the second term is 0). Lower is better. The window is the contiguous run of at least 2 hours (3 samples) with the lowest average score, and it is dropped if that average is above 0.8.

The weights 0.7 and 0.3 and the 0.8 cut-off are fixed numbers with no stated basis.

</DocSection>

<DocSection id="visibility" title="Object visibility graph">

For one object, the graph plots its altitude over the night and the moon curve, and shades the **optimal windows**: the hours when the object is above 30° and the moon curve is below 30. Both thresholds are described in the code as arbitrary. The moon curve is the same unit-less value as above and belongs to the moon alone, so the moon's effect does not depend on how close the moon is to the object.

</DocSection>
